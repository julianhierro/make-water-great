How hard is hard water? How to read your number

Water hardness gets reported in two different units. Here’s how to convert them and what the result means for your house.

November 6, 2026

Ask three people how hard your water is and you might get three different numbers that all mean the same thing. Labs and city reports usually use milligrams per liter (mg/L), which is the same as parts per million. Water treatment people tend to use grains per gallon (gpg).

To convert, divide mg/L by 17.1. So 171 mg/L is 10 gpg.

What the numbers mean

The US Geological Survey uses these bands:

  • 0 to 60 mg/L (under 3.5 gpg): soft
  • 61 to 120 mg/L (3.5 to 7 gpg): moderately hard
  • 121 to 180 mg/L (7 to 10.5 gpg): hard
  • Over 180 mg/L (over 10.5 gpg): very hard

Where to find yours

If you’re on city water, your supplier publishes an annual water quality report, and hardness is often in it. It’s worth checking, but that figure is an average for a whole system and can change with the season and the source. A test at your own tap is more useful. If you’re on a well, there’s no report, so you have to test.

When a softener is worth it

Hard water isn’t a health problem. The case for softening is about your plumbing, your appliances, your skin and how much cleaning you want to do. At the low end you may not notice much. Once you’re in the hard or very hard band, you’ll usually see scale building up within months.

The number also sets the size of the softener. A family of four with 15 gpg water needs a much bigger system than a couple at 8 gpg, and an undersized one regenerates constantly and goes through salt.
